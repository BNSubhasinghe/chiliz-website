Literature survey

What existing research tells us

Precision agriculture research has moved from manual scouting towards sensors, satellites, robots and machine learning. Our survey grouped prior work into six themes that map onto the system's components.

Agricultural ground robots

Unmanned ground vehicles can carry cameras and sensors close to the crop, capturing detail that aerial platforms miss. Most published rovers, however, focus on a single task such as spraying or imaging rather than combined decision support.

Image-based disease detection

Convolutional neural networks with transfer learning, including efficient architectures such as EfficientNet, classify leaf diseases with high accuracy on curated datasets. Explainability methods such as Grad-CAM help users trust predictions, but field images with variable light and occlusion remain challenging.

Soil health prediction

Machine learning models trained on N, P, K, pH and weather variables can classify soil fertility. Sequence models such as LSTM capture patterns across readings, while gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost are strong tabular classifiers, which motivates a hybrid approach.

Smart irrigation

Soil moisture sensors combined with threshold or model-driven control reduce water use compared with fixed-interval schedules. Anomaly detection techniques such as Isolation Forest help separate sensor faults and unusual moisture events from normal drying patterns.

GNSS reliability in the field

GPS and GNSS accuracy degrades under crop canopy, trees and obstructions. Studies fuse GNSS with IMU data or monitor signal quality so a rover can detect unreliable positioning instead of silently drifting off the crop row.

Management zone delineation

Satellite vegetation and water indices such as NDVI, NDWI, EVI and SAVI from Sentinel-2 imagery are widely clustered with K-Means to define management zones, with elbow and silhouette analysis used to choose the number of zones.

Research gap

Strong parts, missing whole

Each capability has been studied, but usually on its own and rarely for chilli. No reviewed approach combined rover navigation reliability, disease and stress monitoring, soil health prediction, smart irrigation and zone-based planning in one farmer-facing system.

Research problem

Damage is found too late

  • Chilli cultivation in Sri Lanka is heavily affected by disease, water stress, soil imbalance and uneven field conditions.
  • Farmers still depend mainly on manual observation, fixed irrigation schedules and delayed soil testing.
  • Early disease symptoms, nutrient deficiencies and moisture stress are often missed until crop damage has already occurred.
  • Ground rover navigation becomes unreliable when GPS and GNSS signals degrade under crop canopy, trees or field obstructions.
  • Existing solutions handle disease detection, irrigation, soil analysis or mapping separately rather than as one integrated system.
Research question

The question we set out to answer

How can an autonomous ground rover-based intelligent system improve chilli crop monitoring and decision-making by integrating disease detection, soil health prediction, irrigation control and field zone mapping?

Research objectives

Objectives

Main objective

To design and develop a Smart Chilli Crop Intelligence System using an autonomous ground rover.

Specific objectives

  • Monitor rover navigation reliability and support sensor-based drip irrigation decisions.
  • Detect chilli crop diseases, stress conditions and growth stages using AI-based image analysis.
  • Predict soil health conditions from real-time soil sensor readings using machine learning.
  • Divide the chilli field into meaningful management zones using spatial clustering.
  • Generate zone-specific recommendations for irrigation, fertiliser use, seeding density and disease treatment.
  • Present all results through a farmer-friendly dashboard for better decision-making.
